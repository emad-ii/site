The Intelligence Age Must Be Built Right

When I left Stability, I said we would not answer centralised AI with more centralised AI.

I knew making the models open mattered. I also knew it was not enough. What I could see was the problem; what I could not yet see was the whole shape of the answer.

The work since has been hard. We have spent two years following one question wherever it led:

Who will own and control AI when it becomes more capable than any of us?

That question is no longer theoretical. AI is moving from answering questions to acting in the world. Our agents will learn, work, earn, spend, contract and direct machines for us. They may help teach our children, manage our health and money, navigate the law and coordinate more of the physical world around us.

Done well, this can put intelligence once reserved for the most powerful institutions within reach of every person.

We had part of the answer at the start. Stable Diffusion showed how quickly capability could spread once people were free to use it, adapt it and build on it.

But an open model can still act through a closed institution. The model may change while the relationship, memory, money and power around it remain concentrated. Opening the models does not tell us who should own the institution through which they act.

At the time, I saw the political problem. The larger answer took longer to understand.

When intelligence becomes infrastructure

The economy we know grew around a simple connection. People worked; work produced income; income carried production back into households and communities. Intelligence was scarce and largely attached to human beings, so production and provision travelled together.

That connection is breaking.

Agents and machines need energy and capital, but they do not need wages or social provision in the way people do. Production can rise while human labour becomes less necessary. An economy may become more efficient without answering who the resulting abundance is for.

The Last Economy begins there. Intelligent Economics shows how the same change appears in the mathematics of human and machine choice.

People and machines do not choose in a vacuum. They act inside a shared world of trust, promises, history and institutions. People and institutions made that world and continue to revise it. Machines increasingly learn from it and may alter it.

Intelligence depends on that shared world. When agents begin to earn, spend and contract, money enters the same change. It records who owns what, who owes what and what resources may be used. The rules around money become part of the rules under which intelligence acts.

As machines take on more production, efficiency no longer guarantees provision. The economic question is who receives the abundance and who keeps the power to direct it.

Economics can show where value moves. It cannot decide what people must retain.

Common Wealth follows the question into common life. A system may teach brilliantly, allocate efficiently or predict more accurately than any person in the room, yet still leave the people living under it unable to inspect, contest or change what it does.

Better is the whole of the trouble. Nobody hands a job to something worse.

Capability does not confer standing. Performance does not confer authority. A machine may reach the right conclusion without acquiring the right to act on it.

This has a plain practical meaning. A person should be able to see what an agent was asked to do, which rule it followed and what happened. If a decision affects them, there must be a named institution able to stop it, repair the harm and hear the appeal. Where the rules themselves are public, people must retain the lawful power to revise them.

If a model company holds our memory, money and permissions, and is the only place we can turn when something goes wrong, it is no longer supplying intelligence alone.

It has become the institution.

Our answer is the Champion.

The Champion

A Champion is a commercial company, formed and owned locally, through which a country or community can use AI in daily life. It is not a government body, even when it serves public institutions.

Its role is to sign the customer contract, hold the balance sheet and operating record, and remain answerable for the service as models, financial providers and machines change. It combines the demand of people and institutions into contracts large enough to bring in suppliers and finance what is needed. It can provide the service, bill for it and settle payments while keeping the relationship and record when one supplier is replaced by another.

This gives a place access to whichever intelligence serves it best without requiring its people, schools, health systems, businesses or public services to surrender their history and relationships to the company supplying the current model.

Local ownership does not create public authority. Any authority a Champion exercises must be given lawfully and remain limited by law, licence or contract. A Champion creates a local institution able to receive that authority without allowing a distant supplier to inherit it by default.

Models and machines become suppliers. The Champion holds the local relationship.

Ownership before value

Each Champion is designed to begin with a local fair launch at $1 pre-money. The dollar is not a forecast of what the company may become. It sets the starting ownership before the company has accumulated value.

The first question is who the institution belongs to, not what outside investors will pay for it. Local people and institutions can join first. Global capital and global suppliers can come later.

The launch can bring much more than cash. It can bring first customers, talent, distribution, credit, institutional relationships and the trust from which contracts become possible. It can attract financial, social and political support at the moment the institution is being formed.

Investment still does not buy authority. A cheque grants neither a public mandate nor the right to bind anybody else.

Ownership begins locally, before value has moved somewhere else.

From models to machines

AI will not stop at the screen. Digital intelligence makes deciding cheaper. Intelligent machines may begin to make doing cheaper.

Most arguments about robots point at the body. One side shows a humanoid doing useful work and says the future has arrived; the other shows it falling over and says it has not. The useful thing is the whole deployment around the machine.

Before a robot can unload a warehouse, work on a building site or help somebody out of bed, someone must finance it, insure it, maintain it, decide where it may work and answer when it fails.

A machine may be made anywhere. It always works somewhere.

Intelligent Machines follows that movement into the physical world. If bodies become cheaper and easier to replace, more of the lasting value may sit in the local work around them. The operator needs to keep the history of how the machine performed, adapt it to the place where it works and retain the right to decide what happens after something goes wrong.

Picture a machine helping a carer move an elderly woman from bed to chair. It can take the weight that damages the carer's back while the carer keeps her eyes and conversation on the person in front of her. The machine should not decide what care the woman receives, who may see through its cameras or where the record of her life goes. Her family needs to know which institution set those rules and who can change them. The manufacturer supplies the body. The local institution answers for its use.

Some of the intelligence inside these systems may also change form. Today much capable AI is either rented remotely or run on general-purpose chips. The Silicon Thesis explores whether intelligence that is used repeatedly and changes slowly can be built into hardware held locally, rather than called from a distant data centre every time.

This will not suit every task, and the engineering still has to be proved. The distinction is useful: put fixed, repeated work into silicon; keep personal memory and changing rules outside it; send genuinely new problems to intelligence that can change.

The chip can change cost, speed and who physically holds the hardware. It does not decide what it may do.

The Champion does not need to manufacture the chip or the machine. It brings together local demand, chooses among competing suppliers, arranges finance, insurance, maintenance and local operation, and remains answerable within the authority it has been given. The manufacturer supplies capability. The Champion holds the relationship in which that capability is used.

Shared systems, local control

Intelligent Internet is building common open systems that Champions can use together without giving up local control.

Zenith carries work across changing models, software and machines. Radiant keeps the sources, permissions, decisions and results so the record survives those changes. The Champion holds the relationship and remains answerable.

The agent can change. The institution remembers.

Each Champion must be able to change any supplier, replace II as the organisation maintaining the software or continue the open systems independently. Leaving has to work in practice: the Champion needs its own records, keys, contracts and people able to operate the system. Open code alone is not independence.

The network is being built to continue without II because we should not answer centralised AI by creating another institution the world cannot leave.

II intends to be paid for helping form Champions and for maintaining the software, connecting suppliers, securing the service and evaluating how it performs. II also intends to hold a stake in each Champion it helps establish, without a right to control it.

Each launch can leave better tools, supplier relationships and operating knowledge for the next. Local records and authority do not need to move for that knowledge to improve. II's place depends on remaining useful. Control remains local.

The choice

Without a locally answerable institution, memory, economic value and decisions about daily life move towards the companies supplying the intelligence. Existing institutions may remain in name while becoming little more than interfaces to systems owned somewhere else. Those systems may be brilliant, efficient and even benevolent.

But the rules will no longer be ours to change.

There is another path. A school can become more capable without handing the child to a model company. A health system can coordinate care without losing responsibility for it. Businesses can use the best models and machines in the world while keeping the relationships that make them valuable. Communities can become vastly more capable without giving away the institution that answers when intelligence acts.

AI does not have to hollow out society. Built correctly, it can raise the capacity of people and institutions while leaving them better able to act, contest and change course.

Two years ago, I knew opening the models was necessary but not enough. We now understand far more clearly what abundance changes, what people need to retain and the kind of institution that can hold both as intelligence moves from software into money and machines.

The next step is to form the first Champions.

The Intelligence Age is arriving either way. We still have time to decide whether the institutions closest to our lives belong to the people they serve, and whether those people keep the last word.
